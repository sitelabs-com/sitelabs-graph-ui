How big a graph

For: developers choosing the page's limits, and anyone wondering why a big answer isn't drawn. You'll find: what was measured, on what, what it shows, and how the page's limits follow from it.

What limits a graph is what you can read, long before what the browser can draw.

What was measured

On an Apple M2 laptop (Chrome, its GPU), on connected data of about 8 relationships per node, all of it drawn:

Nodes, relationships View Layout While laid out Turning or panning Can you read it?
116, 794 3D 5.1 s 60 fps 60 fps Yes
2D 5.0 s 60 fps 61 fps Yes
1,052, 8,032 3D 4.0 s 31 fps 48 fps The clusters only, as a hairball
2D 2.0 s 61 fps 60 fps The clusters only, as a hairball
5,212, 40,198 3D 15.4 s 5 fps 7 fps No: a solid disc
2D 3.5 s 36 fps 55 fps No: a solid disc

Layout is the time until the layout settles (the page records it in the stage's data-laid-out). The two frame-rate columns are while it is settling and afterwards, moving round it.

What it shows

  • Around a hundred nodes read well in either view.
  • Around a thousand still draw smoothly, in 2D especially, but show only the clusters.
  • Five thousand draw (2D comfortably, 3D barely), and show nothing.
  • Focused answers read well at any size of graph. It is the size of the answer that matters, not of the graph.

The page's limits

Limit Value Why
Nodes drawn, by default 1,000 Where a picture stops showing more than clusters
"Hard to read" advice over 500 nodes Before the hairball
"3D is slow" advice over 2,000 nodes in 3D Between 31 and 5 fps
Fewer details drawn over 1,000 nodes No arrows, shorter layout, coarser 3D
Expanding 100 relationships a node One step at a time stays readable

They are constants in ui/console.html (describe(), draw() and EXPAND).

So expanding is the way into a big graph: start from one node and grow it (Expanding a node). Libraries that draw hundreds of thousands of points (cosmos.gl, for example) aren't worth adding for that reason.

Measuring your own

Draw an answer of the size you care about, in each view, and read:

  • the layout time, from the line above the graph ("Laid out in N s"), or document.getElementById('stage').dataset.laidOut in the browser's console;
  • the frame rate, from the browser's performance tools (in Chrome, Rendering → Frame rendering stats), while it settles and while you turn or pan it.

Say the machine, browser and data when adding a row to the table above.
